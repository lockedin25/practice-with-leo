# Leo UI Audit

**Date:** 25 August 2026  
**Scope:** Landing page, case library, sign-in, mobile states, and representative authenticated practice/debrief views.  
**Method:** Rendered local site audit at 1440 × 1000, 1280 × 720, 768 × 1024, and 375 × 812. The practice room and debrief require authentication in the audit browser, so those findings use the supplied authenticated screenshots and should receive a final logged-in verification pass after implementation. This is a UI and interaction audit, not a code review.

## Executive summary

Leo has a credible visual foundation. The restrained evergreen, ivory, and brass palette, paired Newsreader display type, and Plus Jakarta Sans body type create a much more distinctive identity than a generic MBB-prep product. The landing hero is composed and the case library has genuine information richness.

The main issue is not taste. It is hierarchy and restraint. The landing page turns into a second, 69-item case library. The library makes 69 cases feel like a long unprioritised inventory rather than a deliberate practice programme. On mobile, the global navigation and filters are materially too small, and the page overflows horizontally. The authenticated workspace is visually close to the brand but its conversation, evidence, and completion states need a tighter information model.

| Headline score | Grade | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overall design | **B-** | Strong identity and fundamentals, weakened by catalogue density and mobile interaction quality. |
| AI slop risk | **A-** | No purple-gradient template look, no decorative blobs, and typography has a point of view. The risk is excessive repeated cards, not generic styling. |
| Mobile readiness | **C** | Readable, but a 10 px horizontal overflow and undersized touch controls make the core browsing flow feel unfinished. |
| Practice-room usability | **B-** | Clear shell and useful secondary context, but chat measure and completion hierarchy need refinement. |

## First impression

I immediately understand that Leo is an AI case interviewer rather than a generic learning chatbot. My eye goes first to “Practise with Leo”, then the simulated interview panel, then the dark green “Explore Case Library” button. That is the intended hierarchy and it works.

The first screen feels calm, serious, and appropriately premium. The problem begins after the hero. I reach a large catalogue that looks almost identical to the dedicated Case Library, and the page asks me to process dozens of similarly weighted cards. The product begins as a focused coaching experience and gradually feels like an uncurated database.

One-word verdict: **promising**.

## Prioritised findings

### P1. Remove the mobile horizontal overflow

**Evidence:** At 375 px viewport width, the rendered document is 385 px wide. The page can shift sideways by 10 px. See `cases-mobile-fold.png`.

**Why it matters:** Even small horizontal movement reads as broken on a polished consumer product. It also makes right-edge controls easier to clip or miss.

**Recommendation:** Identify the overflowing container and constrain it to the viewport. Treat `document.documentElement.scrollWidth === clientWidth` as a release criterion on the landing page, case library, practice room, profile, and debrief at 375 px.

**Acceptance criteria:** No horizontal page scrolling at 375, 390, 768, 1024, 1280, or 1440 px, in either theme.

### P1. Make navigation and filter controls touch-safe

**Evidence:** On the 375 px library view, the theme toggle is 38 × 38 px; sign-in and primary header actions are 37 px tall; filter chips are 34 px tall. The recommended minimum touch target is 44 × 44 px.

**Why it matters:** These are the first controls a mobile visitor uses. Small controls add accidental taps exactly where the user is trying to choose a case quickly.

**Recommendation:** Preserve the visual compactness but use a 44 px hit area. For the chips, retain a 34 px visible pill if desired, but wrap it in a 44 px interactive target with sufficient spacing.

**Acceptance criteria:** Every interactive element in the mobile header, filter row, and footer has a usable 44 × 44 px hit area. This includes the theme toggle, clear-search control, nav actions, chips, selects, and primary card action.

### P1. Simplify the mobile header and preserve navigation

**Evidence:** The mobile header shows logo, theme toggle, Sign in, and Browse Cases. “Practice Room” and “Your Progress” disappear entirely. See `landing-mobile-fold.png` and `cases-mobile-fold.png`.

**Why it matters:** The desktop navigation explains the product structure. The mobile version turns it into four tight controls and removes a primary destination. A returning user cannot discover progress without first guessing a route or signing in.

**Recommendation:** Keep logo plus one primary action in the header. Put the remaining navigation behind a clearly labelled menu or a compact account/menu control. Once signed in, surface Profile/Progress consistently in that menu.

**Acceptance criteria:** A mobile visitor can reach Home, Case Library, Practice Room, and Progress in one obvious interaction, with no header control below the touch-target minimum.

### P1. Stop duplicating the full library on the landing page

**Evidence:** The landing page begins with a focused hero and then renders the large card catalogue. The same inventory exists on the dedicated Case Library page. The landing page also says “12 Authentic MBB Scenarios” and “View all 12 cases”, while the product visibly contains 69 curated scenarios.

**Why it matters:** The landing page loses its narrative after the hero, becomes extremely long, and gives conflicting signals about what is available. It burdens a first-time visitor with selection before explaining enough of the experience.

**Recommendation:** Limit home to 6 to 12 deliberately curated cases: “Start here”, “Most practised”, or “Recommended for your level”. Move the complete catalogue and its filters exclusively to Case Library. Use one accurate product-count convention everywhere.

**Acceptance criteria:** Landing page contains a curated preview, not all cases. Copy uses one consistent count or clearly differentiates “12 core cases” from “69 total scenarios”.

### P1. Make the library feel like a practice pathway, not an inventory dump

**Evidence:** The library loads 69 visually similar cards. Each card contains source, type, difficulty, description, duration, phase, school, tags, and a CTA. The full-page screenshot reads as a wall of equal-weight rectangles.

**Why it matters:** Cards are necessary here because each is a selectable case, but the present layout gives every metadata field equal importance. It does not help a candidate choose the best next case.

**Recommendation:** Define a strong first-order hierarchy: case name, case type, difficulty, and progress state. Move secondary tags into a compact details view or reveal them on hover/tap. Add a “Recommended next” rail for signed-in users, plus sensible sort defaults such as Recommended, New, Saved, and Difficulty.

**Acceptance criteria:** A user can choose a fitting case in under 10 seconds without reading full descriptions. A card’s primary decision information is visible at a glance, and nonessential metadata does not create a fourth or fifth visual tier.

### P2. Rework the filter system around the actual decision sequence

**Evidence:** Search, seven type chips, school/source, and difficulty appear as one broad control band. The search result state is functional, but a solitary result sits in the left third of a large grid, making the page feel unfinished. See `cases-search-state.png`.

**Why it matters:** The UI makes users decide on case type before it helps them understand why one case is a good fit. School/source is especially secondary for most people.

**Recommendation:** Use search and “Recommended / All cases / Saved” as primary controls. Put type and difficulty in an expandable filter button or a compact horizontal sheet on mobile. Keep source as a secondary filter. In a one-result state, adapt the content width or show a clear “1 result” empty-space treatment rather than leaving an accidental-looking grid gap.

**Acceptance criteria:** Primary controls answer “what should I practise next?”, secondary filters remain discoverable, and narrow result sets have a deliberate layout.

### P2. Fix the missing accessible search-clear affordance

**Evidence:** After searching for “meridian”, a visual × appears in the search field. The accessibility snapshot exposes the search box but no separately named clear-search control.

**Why it matters:** Keyboard and screen-reader users may not be able to identify or operate the clear action. It also makes the control’s behaviour unclear to a mobile user.

**Recommendation:** Use a real button with an accessible label such as “Clear search”, a 44 px mobile hit area, and a visible focus state. Keyboard Escape should clear the current query when the field has focus.

**Acceptance criteria:** The clear action is reachable through keyboard navigation, announced to assistive technology, and has an obvious hover, focus, and pressed state.

### P2. Tighten the dark-mode surface hierarchy

**Evidence:** The landing dark theme is attractive, but the top navigation uses a much lighter charcoal slab than the hero. The authenticated practice screenshots use another near-black shell. This makes light/dark mode feel broadly related rather than governed by one surface-elevation system.

**Why it matters:** Leo should feel like one product across marketing, library, practice, and debrief. Dark mode is where inconsistent blacks and grays are most noticeable.

**Recommendation:** Define three named dark surfaces and use them consistently: app canvas, raised navigation/panel, and interactive card/input. Reserve borders and shadows for separation, not for every layer. Verify muted labels against the actual dark surface rather than relying only on the light theme.

**Acceptance criteria:** The same navigation, card, input, and sidebar surface levels are recognisable on all dark-mode pages; muted body and sidebar copy meets readable contrast in normal use.

### P2. Constrain conversation measure in the practice room

**Evidence:** In the authenticated practice screenshots, Leo and candidate messages can span very wide containers. Several interview turns become long 80+ character lines, while the page still leaves substantial unused space around the transcript.

**Why it matters:** Case work is cognitively demanding. Long line lengths make it harder to scan a premise, a numerical fact, or a recommendation, especially over a 30-minute session.

**Recommendation:** Set a readable max measure for transcript text, approximately 65 to 75 characters per line. Keep the conversation column flexible, but cap bubble text width independently. Use deliberate rhythm between turns instead of changing bubble width based only on text length.

**Acceptance criteria:** Standard interview messages scan comfortably on desktop, tablet, and mobile. No important sentence requires a left-to-right eye sweep across most of the screen.

### P2. Give the evidence sidebar a clearer role and stronger scan hierarchy

**Evidence:** The practice screenshots show an Interview Progress card and an Evidence Released card. The evidence cards carry dense all-caps identifiers and full paragraph text in a narrow right rail.

**Why it matters:** Evidence is crucial to the case, but the sidebar currently feels like a simultaneous transcript rather than a usable working memory aid. The reader must scan dense mini-cards while also following the conversation.

**Recommendation:** Make the most recently released fact visibly primary. Reduce identifier prominence, add one-line summaries, and allow a card to expand for full detail. Consider a compact “new since your last turn” indicator. On tablet, move evidence into a bottom sheet or dedicated tab rather than forcing a narrow rail.

**Acceptance criteria:** A candidate can identify the latest fact, its category, and its implication without rereading every evidence card. The tablet layout does not make the right rail too narrow to read.

### P2. Improve the completion state’s action hierarchy

**Evidence:** The debrief-complete screenshot shows the message “Practice complete” with a primary “View Full Debrief Report” action and a “New Case” action in the same dense bottom bar. The lower bar competes with the final conversation content and visually arrives abruptly.

**Why it matters:** The moment immediately after finishing is where the product needs to convert effort into learning. The report should be unmistakably the next step, while starting another case should be available but secondary.

**Recommendation:** Turn completion into a discrete, calm summary panel with one primary action: “Review your debrief”. Include a short outcome snapshot such as overall score, strongest skill, and one next action. Keep “Start another case” as a secondary text action.

**Acceptance criteria:** A user understands that the case has ended and what to do next within two seconds. The primary debrief action has unambiguous visual dominance and remains easy to tap on mobile.

### P2. Eliminate unauthenticated console errors from public pages

**Evidence:** Landing, library, and sign-in all trigger unauthenticated `401` requests for user/profile data in the audit browser. The pages render, but the browser console reports errors on every public page load.

**Why it matters:** This is not necessarily visible to an end user, but it hides genuine front-end errors during QA and creates unnecessary noise in monitoring.

**Recommendation:** Treat anonymous identity and anonymous case-progress state as an expected state rather than an error path. Avoid user-specific requests before sign-in, or handle the expected unauthenticated response without emitting a console error.

**Acceptance criteria:** Fresh anonymous visits to `/`, `/cases`, and `/login` show no console errors.

### P3. Reduce instruction and metadata density where the UI already communicates intent

**Evidence:** The landing library section, case cards, sign-in checklist, progress sidebar, and practice badges each layer labels, tags, and explanatory copy. Individually these are sensible; collectively they make the product sound more explanatory than confident.

**Why it matters:** Leo’s strongest visual quality is its calmness. Repeating labels and taxonomy spends that calmness too freely.

**Recommendation:** Keep labels that change a decision or confirm a system state. Remove or demote labels that simply repeat the heading, card type, or CTA. In particular, audit badge and tag density before adding more content.

**Acceptance criteria:** Each screen has one clear task, one primary action, and only the metadata required to complete that task.

## Cross-page consistency

### Strong

- The core palette and typography are recognisably Leo across public and authenticated screenshots.
- The case-interview motif is clear without relying on stock imagery or gimmicks.
- Light mode feels editorial rather than SaaS-template generic.
- The sign-in page is focused and calm, with a clearly dominant Google action.

### Needs consolidation

- Public pages use an editorial marketing rhythm; the practice room is a dense utility interface. That is appropriate, but the surface, sidebar, and control rules should be visibly related.
- Primary CTA wording varies among “Browse Cases”, “Explore Case Library”, “Enter Practice”, “Begin case”, and “Start practice”. These are all understandable, but the product should use a smaller intentional CTA vocabulary.
- Case counts and the relationship between core cases and total scenarios need a single content rule.

## Interaction flow review

### Flow: Landing to case selection

I land on a convincing hero and can immediately browse cases. That is good. I then reach a huge card wall before I have been given a recommendation or a simple first decision. My goodwill drops because the product seems to hand the curation problem back to me.

**Goodwill: 62 / 100**

```text
Landing hero                 70 → 80  (+10: purpose and primary action are immediately clear)
Full catalogue on landing    80 → 65  (-15: too much selection before guidance)
Case Library filters         65 → 62  (-3: functional but too many equal controls)
Search response              62 → 67  (+5: fast and understandable)
Mobile tap targets           67 → 62  (-5: compact controls increase friction)
```

### Flow: Case completion to learning

I can see that the interview has finished, and the debrief CTA exists. But the bottom bar does not yet create a satisfying “you did the work, here is what you learned” moment. It feels like a transactional footer rather than the payoff of the practice session.

## Recommended implementation order

1. **Mobile foundation:** eliminate horizontal overflow, raise all touch targets to 44 px, and add an intentional mobile navigation pattern.
2. **Information architecture:** remove the full catalogue from landing, define consistent case-count language, and make the library’s default state recommendation-led.
3. **Library card system:** reduce visible metadata and add a clear primary decision hierarchy with progress states.
4. **Practice-room reading:** cap transcript measure and redesign evidence for quick scanning, especially at tablet width.
5. **Completion payoff:** redesign the debrief-complete state around a calm, single next step.
6. **Polish and QA:** consolidate dark surface tokens, improve search-clear accessibility, and remove expected anonymous-request console errors.

## Quick wins

These should each be independently small changes once the relevant source is opened:

1. Remove the 10 px mobile overflow and add viewport regression checks.
2. Increase header and filter hit areas to 44 px without changing their visual pill size.
3. Replace “View all 12 cases” with accurate, specific copy.
4. Add an accessible label and focus state to the search-clear action.
5. Suppress expected unauthenticated console errors on public routes.

## Final verification checklist

Before implementation is considered complete, repeat this audit while signed in and check:

- Case library with `not started`, `in progress`, and `completed` states.
- Practice room during a long transcript, a fact release, voice on/off, and completion.
- Profile with no attempts, several attempts, and a low-progress user.
- Debrief on desktop, tablet, and 375 px mobile.
- Keyboard tab order, visible focus, and screen-reader labels for all custom controls.
