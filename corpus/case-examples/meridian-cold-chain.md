# Meridian Cold Chain: Case Example

## Case at a glance

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Client | Meridian Cold Chain, an Indian temperature-controlled logistics operator |
| Customers | Manufacturers of packaged food and pharmaceutical products |
| Footprint | Pan-India |
| Case type | Profitability |
| Core metric | EBITDA margin |
| Problem | EBITDA margin fell from about 18% to about 11% over two years, despite steady revenue growth |
| CEO's decision | Identify the cause of the decline and restore economics over the next 12 months |

## Client and business model

Meridian transports temperature-sensitive consignments for manufacturers under contracted transport arrangements. It earns revenue for moving customers' goods, generally measured on a per-kilogram basis in the case data.

The economics of a route depend on three linked factors:

- which consignments can travel together;
- the required temperature control and delivery reliability; and
- how much of the vehicle's carrying capacity is used.

Meridian serves two distinct businesses:

| Segment | Operating character |
|---|---|
| Packaged food | Largely recurring replenishment flows. Demand is relatively predictable, so compatible loads can usually be planned together on regular routes. |
| Pharmaceuticals | Less standardised and more sensitive to reliable handling, temperature integrity, and delivery timing. A commercially attractive shipment can still be operationally expensive to serve. |

## The performance problem

Revenue has grown by about 20% over the two-year period and price per kilogram has been broadly stable. The deterioration is therefore on the cost-to-serve side rather than driven by lower prices or weaker volume.

There were no acquisitions, impairments, or other one-off charges in either year. Meridian operates nationwide, but no individual region explains the decline. Food-only competitors maintained their margins over the same period, indicating that this is not a broad packaged-food industry margin collapse.

## Business mix shift

The principal change in Meridian's business has been its product mix.

| Segment | Share of volume two years ago | Share of volume today | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Packaged food | 95% | 70% | -25 percentage points |
| Pharmaceuticals | 5% | 30% | +25 percentage points |

Pharmaceutical growth is therefore large enough to materially affect Meridian's blended economics.

## Current segment economics

| Segment | Share of volume | Revenue per kg (INR) | Cost per kg (INR) | Contribution per kg (INR) | Contribution margin |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Packaged food | 70% | 8.0 | 6.4 | 1.6 | 20.0% |
| Pharmaceuticals | 30% | 14.0 | 14.0 | 0.0 | 0.0% |

Pharma commands a higher selling price, but it earns no contribution per kilogram. By contrast, each kilogram of food earns INR 1.6 of contribution. The pharma mix shift therefore dilutes the company's blended margin even as revenue grows.

Using the current mix, the supplied economics reconcile to an approximately 11.4% blended contribution margin:

| Calculation | Result |
|---|---:|
| Volume-weighted contribution per kg: (70% × 1.6) + (30% × 0.0) | INR 1.12 |
| Volume-weighted revenue per kg: (70% × 8.0) + (30% × 14.0) | INR 9.80 |
| Blended contribution margin: 1.12 / 9.80 | 11.4% |

This is directionally consistent with the reported EBITDA margin of about 11%. The case does not provide a fixed-versus-variable cost ledger, so this segment view is the most useful available economic cut; it should not be treated as a full accounting bridge from contribution to EBITDA.

## Operating mechanism behind pharma's cost base

| Operating factor | Packaged food | Pharmaceuticals | Economic implication |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Route load factor | About 88% | About 55% | Lower pharma utilisation spreads trip costs across fewer kilograms, raising cost per kg. |
| Shipment pattern | Predictable, recurring replenishment | Smaller and time-critical consignments | Pharma loads are harder to pool efficiently. |
| Temperature requirement | Wider band tolerated | 2°C to 8°C | Pharma loads generally cannot be combined with food on the same vehicle. |
| Service requirements | Regular route planning | High delivery and handling reliability | Efficiency initiatives cannot compromise temperature integrity or delivery reliability. |

The key constraint is not simply poor route planning. Pharma's service requirements limit consolidation with food, so a recommendation to fill vehicles by mixing the two businesses would be operationally invalid.

An illustrative sensitivity in the supplied case assumes pharma cost per kilogram falls in proportion to better utilisation. At a 85% load factor, the calculation is INR 14.0 × 55 / 85 = approximately INR 9.1 per kg. This illustrates why route density matters, but it is not a commitment that all pharma routes can reach that load factor; time-critical service constraints may prevent it.

## Commercial and contract context

Meridian priced its pharma contracts by taking the food cost base and adding a premium. It did not re-cost the work to reflect pharma's lower load factor. This is the commercial error that turns a difficult operating model into zero-contribution business.

The contracts offer a practical opening for action:

- Pharma contracts permit an annual price revision with 90 days' notice.
- Two of Meridian's four largest pharma contracts reopen within six months.
- For future pharma work, prices should reflect lane-specific cost-to-serve, temperature requirements, service standards, and shipment size.

## Root cause

Meridian's EBITDA margin fell because its volume mix shifted sharply into pharmaceuticals, a segment whose low load factors make it much more expensive to serve per kilogram. Meridian had priced that work using the packaged-food cost base rather than its actual cost-to-serve. As a result, pharma earns no contribution per kilogram and dilutes the company-wide margin as it becomes a larger share of volume.

## Recommended 12-month response

| Priority | Action | Why it matters | Key risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Re-cost every pharma lane and reprice the contracts that reopen within six months. | Addresses the direct cause: the current price does not cover the actual cost base. | A large customer may leave, removing revenue faster than cost can be removed. |
| 2 | Improve density on viable pharma-only routes through consolidation within pharma and scheduled milk runs on the densest lanes. | A fuller vehicle reduces trip cost per kilogram. | Extra stops or waiting time can compromise time-critical service levels. |
| 3 | Introduce a minimum-consignment charge and an explicit temperature/service premium in all new pharma quotations. | Prevents the same underpricing from recurring as the pharma business grows. | Smaller customers may be priced out, slowing segment growth. |
| 4 | Hold or selectively exit pharma volume that cannot be repriced to an acceptable contribution level. | Revenue growth that does not cover its cost-to-serve damages margin. | Reducing volume can affect customer relationships and fleet utilisation. |

## Immediate next steps

1. Build a lane-by-lane pharma cost-to-serve model, including load factor, temperature requirements, delivery windows, shipment size, and service commitments.
2. Prioritise the two large contracts that reopen within six months; prepare revised prices and a retention plan for the most price-sensitive customers.
3. Map pharma-only route density to identify lanes where consolidation or scheduled milk runs can improve utilisation without weakening service quality.
4. Put a commercial approval gate in place so every new pharma bid includes a minimum charge and explicit premiums for temperature and time-critical requirements.
5. Track price realisation, contribution per kilogram, pharma load factor, customer retention, on-time delivery, and temperature-compliance incidents monthly.

## Decision guardrails

- Do not combine food and pharma loads merely to raise fill rates: their temperature and service requirements are generally incompatible.
- Do not judge pharma attractiveness from its price per kilogram alone; assess contribution after its true cost-to-serve.
- Do not assume a regional fix: no single geography is driving the margin decline.
- Do not pursue volume growth before ensuring the incremental work earns a positive contribution.

## One-sentence case answer

Re-cost and reprice Meridian's pharma business first, then improve viable pharma-only route density and change quote discipline, because the move from food to underpriced, low-utilisation pharma work is what reduced blended EBITDA margin from about 18% to about 11%.
