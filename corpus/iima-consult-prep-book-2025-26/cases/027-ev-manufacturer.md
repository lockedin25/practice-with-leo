# EV Manufacturer

## Opening brief

Your client is an automotive company looking to launch into the EV space. They want you to help them decide when they should start electric vehicle development.

## Facilitator interview script

> Speaker labels are reconstructed from the source slide’s shaded interviewer callouts. `Source sequence` marks a turn that needs human speaker review before runtime approval.

<!-- Source PDF page 99, column 1 -->

### Turn 1 - Candidate

I want to restate the requirement for clarity. My client is an automotive company that currently does not have any product in the EV segment. They are looking to enter the same and want us to determine the right time to do so.

### Turn 2 - Interviewer

Yes, that's correct. To elaborate further, they don't want to develop too early when there is insufficient demand as there would be costs of unsold inventory. Neither do they want to miss the early mover advantage. They want to time the launch as closely as possible with the rise in demand.

### Turn 3 - Candidate

Okay, can we assume that the client has done the required research and is in a position to accurately calculate the time to market?

### Turn 4 - Interviewer

Yes, that's a fair assumption.

### Turn 5 - Candidate

So, I'd like to start with a few preliminary questions. Which geographies does the client operate in? What is the product portfolio of the client? What segments of the market do they currently operate in, and what is the company's market share in each of them? What does the competitive scenario look like?

### Turn 6 - Interviewer

For simplicity, assume there's just one market where your client commands 50% of the share in the 4-wheeler category. The remaining is split almost equally among 3-4 competitors. The existing product portfolio is not relevant to the case, so feel free to assume anything.

### Turn 7 - Candidate

Got it. Is the new product being launched in the 2-wheeler or 4-wheeler segment?

### Turn 8 - Interviewer

It is going to be in the 4-wheeler segment.

### Turn 9 - Candidate

All right. Since we want to time the launch with the rise in demand, I want to start by analyzing the types of demand. I would split demand into want-based and mandated / need-based demand. The former refers to the value derived from owning an EV, such as social status and/or public fame. In contrast, the latter refers to demand generated due to mandatory laws or economic viability. Does that look reasonable?

### Turn 10 - Candidate

Yes, it does.

### Turn 11 - Candidate

Great. So, we can now delve deeper. The want-based demand refers to the 'total value of ownership' and is only relevant if owning an EV is not mainstream. Thus, it does not significantly contribute to the kind of demand we're interested in; we can focus only on the need-based demand.

### Turn 12 - Candidate

That's a great insight. Please continue.

<!-- Source PDF page 99, column 2 -->

### Turn 13 - Candidate

Okay, so need-based demand would reach desirable levels when the 'total cost of ownership' of an EV is lower than that of a regular vehicle. This will happen in two scenarios:

### Turn 14 - Candidate

1. if there are government-implemented policies, which includes:

### Turn 15 - Candidate

a. Tax breaks on EVs

### Turn 16 - Candidate

b. Fines on regular vehicles

### Turn 17 - Candidate

2. if the natural cost of an EV falls below that of a regular vehicle

### Turn 18 - Interviewer

That makes sense. How do you want to proceed now?

### Turn 19 - Candidate

Unless we have more information about government-implemented policies timelines, I would like to proceed with analyzing it basis the natural cost of EV.

### Turn 20 - Interviewer

How can one calculate the natural cost of an EV?

### Turn 21 - Candidate

So, we can split it into an initial fixed cost and a running variable cost. Fixed cost consists of the purchase cost of the vehicle, while the running variable cost includes charging, power and maintenance costs.

### Turn 22 - Interviewer

Good. Now imagine that an electronic car costs 50% more than a regular one, x, but has 1/3rd the running cost, y (including power, maintenance etc.). What kind of consumer would prefer the EV?

### Turn 23 - Candidate

Okay. In that case (3/2x + 1/3y) should be less than x + y. Solving the equation, 3/4x should be less than y.

### Turn 24 - Interviewer

That makes sense. What is the inference that you can draw from this information?

### Turn 25 - Candidate

The consumer would prefer an EV if the total cost is less than a regular one. On calculation, it is a better decision if the consumer's lifetime usage of the car exceeds 3/4 times the ratio of the fixed and running cost of the regular car (non-EV).

### Turn 26 - Interviewer

Sounds good. Let's end the case.
